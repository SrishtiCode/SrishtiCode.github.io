SNARK vs. STARK

Both let a prover convince a verifier that a computation was performed correctly without forcing the verifier to repeat the entire computation. The important difference is how they achieve that succinct verification.

Srishti Rathi · Technical Notes

Start with the problem

Suppose I execute a large computation and get some result. Instead of asking you to execute the whole program again, I want to give you a cryptographic proof that the computation was performed correctly.

computation → witness / execution → proof → verifier → accept / reject

SNARKs and STARKs are two major approaches to building such proof systems. They share the same broad goal, but their cryptographic machinery and engineering trade-offs are different.

What is a SNARK?

SNARK stands for Succinct Non-interactive Argument of Knowledge. “Succinct” means that the proof is small and verification is much cheaper than repeating the original computation. “Non-interactive” means that after the proof is produced, the verifier can check it without an ongoing conversation with the prover.

Many well-known SNARK constructions transform computation into algebraic constraints and ultimately into statements about polynomials. Depending on the proving system, polynomial commitments and elliptic-curve cryptography can then be used to create a compact proof.

program → constraints → polynomials → commitments → succinct proof

Why are SNARK proofs so small?

A verifier does not receive every intermediate value from the computation. The proving system compresses the claim into a small cryptographic proof. In pairing-based systems such as Groth16, the verifier can check a few elliptic-curve elements using pairing equations rather than replaying the computation.

The trade-off is that some SNARK constructions require a trusted setup. If secret setup randomness is retained and compromised, the security of that particular setup can fail. This is not a property of every SNARK, so “SNARK = trusted setup” should not be treated as a universal rule.

What is a STARK?

STARK stands for Scalable Transparent Argument of Knowledge. The word transparent is one of the most important differences: STARK constructions avoid a secret trusted setup.

A STARK commonly represents a computation as an execution trace. Algebraic constraints describe how valid rows of that trace must relate to each other. Those constraints are transformed into polynomial identities, committed to with hashes, and checked using low-degree testing.

execution trace → AIR constraints → polynomials → Merkle commitments → FRI

Instead of sending an entire polynomial to the verifier, the prover commits to evaluations of the polynomial. The verifier then checks a small number of positions and uses a protocol such as FRI to gain confidence that the committed evaluations correspond to a polynomial of sufficiently low degree.

The practical comparison

Property SNARK STARK
Proof size Typically very small Typically larger
Verification Usually very fast Fast, but proofs carry more data
Trusted setup Required by some constructions Transparent; no secret setup
Common machinery Polynomial commitments, often elliptic curves Finite fields, hashes, Merkle trees, FRI
Post-quantum direction Depends on the construction Hash-based STARKs avoid elliptic-curve assumptions
Prover scaling Depends strongly on the proving system Designed around large computations and scalable proving
“SNARK” and “STARK” are families, not single protocols. Comparing them always hides details: Groth16, PLONK-style systems, and different STARK implementations can have very different performance characteristics.

The biggest conceptual difference

A useful way to build intuition is to look at what gives the verifier confidence.

In many pairing-based SNARKs, succinct verification comes from algebraic commitments and elliptic-curve pairing relations. In a STARK, the verifier relies heavily on polynomial identities, cryptographic hashes, Merkle commitments, random sampling, and low-degree testing.

SNARK: algebraic constraints → polynomial commitment machinery → compact verification
STARK: execution trace → polynomial constraints → commitments + FRI → probabilistic verification

Why STARK proofs are larger

STARK verification usually needs authentication paths for queried values, polynomial evaluations, and information from FRI rounds. Merkle authentication paths consist of multiple hashes, so the proof naturally contains more data.

That larger proof is part of the trade-off for transparency and a hash-oriented design.

Which one should you use?

There is no universal winner. If extremely small proofs and cheap on-chain verification dominate your requirements, a SNARK construction may be attractive. If transparency, hash-based assumptions, and scalable proving are priorities, a STARK may be a better fit.

In real systems the decision also depends on prover time, verifier cost, recursion, hardware, proof aggregation, implementation maturity, and where verification happens.

Takeaway

SNARKs and STARKs solve the same high-level problem: proving computation without making the verifier repeat it.

The interesting difference is not simply “small proof versus big proof.” It is the cryptographic path each family takes to make computation verifiable.

Understanding constraints, polynomials, commitments, and low-degree testing makes that difference much easier to see.
