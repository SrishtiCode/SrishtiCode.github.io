What Psychology of Intelligence Analysis Taught Me About Thinking

Date: December 2025

PART I — OUR MENTAL MACHINERY

Chapter 1: Thinking About Thinking

When I first started reading this book, as it was recommended by some experts in CTI, I initially wondered why I was reading a psychology book instead of learning more about security. At that time, I was unaware that this exact book would give me the answer to how our assumptions and biases affect our thinking process, and how this further shapes the mindset and mental model of an analyst.

When we begin learning or researching a topic, we should always start with an open mind rather than a prior decision already formed. In the first chapter, I learned about the role of the observer in determining what is observed and how it is interpreted, and why intelligence analysts must understand themselves before they can understand others.

Most intelligence analyst training focuses on organizational procedures, methodological techniques, or substantive topics. Very little training is directed inward—toward the analyst’s own thinking process. It is often assumed, incorrectly, that analysts already know how to analyze.

Mindsets always exist within an analyst’s mind, and they have both advantages and disadvantages. While mindsets help analysts work efficiently during stable periods, they can also blind even experienced specialists when events take an unexpected turn.

One of the most important lessons from this chapter is that the Intelligence Community often reacts to analytical uncertainty by collecting more information—even when analysts already have more data than they can process. What analysts truly need is better reasoning, better mental models, and more relevant intelligence, especially reliable HUMINT.

In summary, analysts may recognize problems in organizations or groups, but it is extremely difficult to be self-aware about the limitations of one’s own thinking. This chapter reinforced that improving intelligence analysis begins with improving how analysts think.

Key Takeaways

  • Intelligence failures often stem from flawed thinking, not lack of data
  • Analysts interpret information through assumptions and mental models
  • Expertise can become a weakness during unexpected change
  • Better analysis requires self-awareness and structured thinking
