What Psychology of Intelligence Analysis Taught Me About Thinking

Date: December 2025

PART I—OUR MENTAL MACHINERY

Chapter 1 (Thinking About Thinking)

When I first started reading this book, as it was recommended by some experts in CTI, I initially wondered why I was reading a psychology book instead of learning more about security. At that time, I was unaware that this exact book would give me the answer to how our assumptions and biases affect our process of thinking about a topic, and how this further shapes the mindset and mental model of an analyst. When we start learning or researching a topic, we should always begin with an open mind rather than with a prior decision already formed. In the first chapter, I learned about the role of the observer in determining what is observed and how it is interpreted, and why intelligence analysts must understand themselves before they can understand others.

Most of the time, training for intelligence analysts focuses on organizational procedures, methodological techniques, or substantive topics. Not enough training is focused inward—toward the analyst’s own thought processes. There should be more training time devoted to the mental act of thinking and analyzing. It is often assumed, incorrectly, that analysts already know how to analyze.

There is always some kind of mindset present in a person’s mind, and it has both advantages and disadvantages. The disadvantage of a mindset is that it can color and control our perception to the extent that even an experienced specialist may be among the last to recognize what is really happening when events take a new and unexpected turn. The advantage of mindsets is that they help analysts get production out on time and keep things running effectively between those watershed events that later become chapter headings in history books.

One of the major things I learned is that the common reaction of the Intelligence Community to many problems is to collect more information, even though analysts often already have more information than they can digest. What analysts truly need is more useful information—especially reliable HUMINT from knowledgeable insiders—to help them make better decisions.

In summary, analysts can often observe and understand problems in small-group and organizational processes, but it is extremely difficult to be self-conscious about the workings of one’s own mind. This chapter made me realize that improving intelligence analysis starts with improving how we think.

Thank you for reading.
